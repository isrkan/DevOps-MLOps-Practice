# 1.2 The Machine Learning Lifecycle

Building an AI capability — whether a traditional ML model or a generative AI application — follows a consistent set of stages. Understanding this lifecycle helps business leaders set realistic timelines, ask the right questions of their technical teams, and identify where investments in process, tooling, or people are most needed.

This document covers the five core stages of the ML lifecycle, the Google Cloud tools that support each stage, and a framework for choosing the right foundation model for our specific needs.

---

## Why the Lifecycle Matters for Business Leaders
It is tempting to think of AI as "you turn it on and it works." In practice, getting from a business problem to a working, production-quality AI system involves a disciplined sequence of steps — and each step has ways to succeed and ways to fail.

Most AI projects that don't deliver value fail at one of three stages:
1. **Data:** The data needed doesn't exist, is poor quality, or can't be accessed
2. **Deployment:** The model works in testing but breaks in production due to real-world variation
3. **Management:** The model works at launch but degrades over time as the world changes and nobody is monitoring it

A clear view of the lifecycle lets us anticipate and prevent these failures.

---

## The Five Stages of the ML Lifecycle

```
Data Ingestion → Data Preparation → Model Training → Model Deployment → Model Management
```

Each stage must be done well for the next to succeed. Problems at early stages propagate forward and become progressively more expensive to fix.

---

### Stage 1: Data Ingestion (Data Gathering)
**What happens:** Data from various sources is collected, moved, and made available for AI use. This includes structured data from databases, unstructured data from file systems, real-time streams from operational systems, and third-party data from external providers.

**Why it matters:** AI is only as good as its training data. Before any model can be built, the raw data must be accessible, organized, and of sufficient volume and variety. Data ingestion is the foundation of the entire lifecycle — and it is often the most underestimated stage in terms of effort.

In practice, organizations often discover at this stage that:
- Data exists in separate, incompatible systems that have never been integrated
- Data is stored in formats that require significant transformation before use
- Data governance rules restrict what data can be used for AI training
- Real-time data needs are more complex than batch data collection

**Google Cloud tools at this stage:**

| Tool | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Cloud Storage** | Object storage for files of any type — documents, images, audio, video. The most common staging area for raw data. |
| **BigQuery** | Enterprise data warehouse for large-scale structured and semi-structured data. Exceptionally fast for analytical queries across huge datasets. |
| **Pub/Sub** | Real-time message streaming — ingesting high-velocity data from sensors, applications, user events, or external systems. |
| **Dataflow** | Managed pipeline service for both batch and streaming data processing. Handles the movement and initial transformation of large data volumes. |
| **Cloud SQL** | Fully managed relational database engine (supporting PostgreSQL, MySQL, and SQL Server). Ideal for ingesting and handling standard operational and transactional data from business applications. |
| **Spanner** | Enterprise-grade, globally continuous relational database. Built for unlimited scale and strong consistency across regions, making it the choice for high-velocity global transactions and mission-critical operational data. |

### Stage 2: Data Preparation
**What happens:** Raw data is cleaned, validated, transformed, labeled, and made ready for model training. This is often the most time-consuming stage — and the one with the most leverage over model quality.

Data preparation includes:
- **Cleaning:** Removing duplicates, correcting errors, handling missing values
- **Normalization:** Converting data into consistent formats (date formats, units of measurement, categorical encodings)
- **Labeling:** For supervised learning, annotating examples with correct answers (this is expensive and time-consuming)
- **Feature engineering:** For traditional ML, selecting and transforming the variables the model will learn from
- **Splitting:** Dividing data into training, validation, and test sets to enable proper evaluation

**Why it matters:** An often-cited industry estimate holds that data scientists spend 60–80% of their time on data preparation rather than model development. The quality of our training data directly determines the ceiling of what our model can achieve — a model trained on clean, well-labeled, representative data will significantly outperform one trained on messy data, regardless of how sophisticated the model architecture is.

| **Vertex AI Data Labeling Service** | Manages human-in-the-loop annotation workflows, allowing us to seamlessly distribute raw files (images, unstructured text, video) to human raters to generate clean target tags and training ground truths. |
| **Dataflow** | Large-scale data transformation pipelines — converting, filtering, aggregating, and routing data at scale. |

### Stage 3: Model Training
**What happens:** The prepared data is used to train a model — adjusting the model's parameters so that it performs well on the task. For traditional ML, this means training a model from scratch on our labeled data. For GenAI, it more commonly means fine-tuning a foundation model on our data to adapt it for our use case.

Training is computationally intensive — it requires significant computing resources (CPUs, GPUs, or TPUs), and for large models, significant time. A key decision at this stage is hardware: traditional CPUs for lightweight models, GPUs for most ML workloads, and Google's TPUs (Tensor Processing Units) for very large models where training speed and cost efficiency matter most.

**Why it matters:** Training is where the "intelligence" of the model is built. However, for most GenAI use cases, organizations are not training models from scratch — they are adapting pre-trained foundation models. This dramatically reduces the compute requirements, data needs, and specialized expertise required compared to full training.

**Google Cloud tools at this stage:**

| Tool | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Models (Vertex AI Training)** | Managed training service — submit training jobs at scale without managing the underlying compute infrastructure. Supports custom containers. |
| **Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform Workbench (Vertex AI Workbench)** | Managed Jupyter notebooks in the cloud — for exploratory work, prototyping, and interactive model development. |
| **TPUs (Tensor Processing Units)** | Google's custom AI accelerator chips — designed for matrix operations at the heart of neural network training. Dramatically faster and more cost-efficient than GPUs for large models. |
| **GPUs** | Standard accelerators for ML workloads. Available in various configurations through Compute Engine and Vertex AI. |

### Stage 4: Model Deployment
**What happens:** The trained model is made available for use — accepting inputs and returning predictions or generated content in response. Deployment involves packaging the model, exposing it through an API or application interface, and configuring the serving infrastructure for the expected load.

Key decisions at deployment:
- **Real-time vs. batch:** Should the model respond to individual requests immediately (real-time inference), or process large batches of inputs on a schedule (batch prediction)?
- **Latency requirements:** How fast does the model need to respond? Customer-facing applications typically need responses in milliseconds to seconds; back-office batch jobs can take longer.
- **Scaling:** How many simultaneous requests must the system handle? This determines the required infrastructure.
- **Integration:** How does the deployed model connect to the applications and workflows that will use it?

**Why it matters:** A model that works perfectly in a testing environment frequently encounters problems in production — real-world inputs look different from training data, traffic volumes are unpredictable, and integration with existing systems introduces complexity. Deployment is where many AI projects encounter their most practical challenges.

**Google Cloud tools at this stage:**

| Tool | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Agent Runtime** | Managed serving layer within the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform. Built for rapid auto-scaling, sub-second cold starts, and hosting both real-time model endpoints and long-running autonomous workflows. |
| **Cloud Run** | Serverless container execution — for models packaged as APIs that need to scale to zero during low-traffic periods. Cost-efficient for variable workloads. |
| **Google Kubernetes Engine (GKE)** | Container orchestration for complex, high-scale deployments where you need fine-grained control over the serving infrastructure. |

### Stage 5: Model Management
**What happens:** The deployed model is monitored, evaluated, maintained, and eventually updated or replaced. This stage is ongoing — it never ends as long as the model is in production.

Model management includes:
- **Performance monitoring:** Is the model still producing accurate, useful outputs? Are there signs of drift?
- **Data drift detection:** Has the distribution of inputs changed since training in ways that degrade performance?
- **Retraining triggers:** When should the model be retrained on newer data?
- **Version management:** Tracking which version of the model is in production and how it compares to alternatives
- **Feature management:** Ensuring the features (data inputs) the model relies on are consistently computed and available

**The problem of model drift:** Models trained on historical data may gradually become less accurate as the world changes. A fraud detection model trained in 2022 needs to be updated to catch 2025 fraud patterns. A customer service model trained before a major product change may give outdated answers. Without active monitoring, models silently degrade while appearing to still function.

**Why it matters:** The value of an AI system is not realized at launch — it is realized over its operational lifetime. An organization that invests in deployment but not in ongoing management will see its AI investments erode in quality over time, sometimes without realizing it.

## Choosing the Right Foundation Model
When building a GenAI application, one of the first and most consequential decisions is which foundation model to use. There is no universally "best" model — the right choice depends on our specific requirements. The following criteria provide a structured framework for evaluation.

---

### Criterion 1: Modality
**What it means:** What types of content does our use case involve — text, images, audio, video, code, or some combination?

- If our application involves only text — reading documents, answering questions, writing content — a text-specialized model may perform better than a multimodal one for that specific task.
- If our application involves multiple content types — analyzing images alongside text, processing audio, generating video — we need a multimodal model.
- If our use case involves code generation or code explanation, models with specific code training (like CodeGemma) may outperform general language models.

**Business example:** A legal document review tool that only processes text contracts is well-served by a strong text model. A customer service system that handles photos of damaged products alongside text complaints requires multimodal capability.

---

### Criterion 2: Context Window
**What it means:** The context window is the maximum amount of text (or equivalent tokens) the model can process in a single interaction. Everything we want the model to consider — our question, background documents, conversation history, instructions — must fit within the context window.

| Context Window Size | What It Can Handle |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| 8K–32K tokens | Individual documents, typical conversation threads |
| 128K tokens | Lengthy reports, book chapters, extended codebases |
| 1M+ tokens | Entire books, massive codebases, complete meeting archives |

**Business example:** A tool that needs to analyze a complete 300-page regulatory filing in one pass requires a model with a very large context window. A chatbot answering simple product questions can work effectively with a smaller window.

---

### Criterion 3: Security and Compliance
**What it means:** How does the model handle our data? Where does it go, who can see it, and what guarantees does the provider offer?

Key questions:
- Does the API provider use our prompts and data to retrain their models? (Most enterprise tiers explicitly do not.)
- What data residency options exist? (Can our data stay in a specific geographic region for compliance?)
- What compliance certifications does the platform hold? (SOC 2, ISO 27001, HIPAA BAA availability, FedRAMP)
- For highly sensitive data: is an on-premises or private deployment option available?

**Business example:** A healthcare organization processing patient information may require a model deployment that qualifies under HIPAA, with a Business Associate Agreement from the provider. A financial services firm may require data residency guarantees specific to their regulatory jurisdiction. An organization with classified data may need an air-gapped deployment using an open-weight model like Gemma on their own infrastructure.

---

### Criterion 4: Availability and Reliability
**What it means:** What service level agreements (SLAs) does the model provider offer, and can it meet our application's uptime and latency requirements?

- **Uptime:** What percentage of the time is the API available? For customer-facing applications, even brief outages are costly.
- **Latency:** How long does the model take to respond? An internal productivity tool can tolerate a few seconds; a live customer support application may need sub-second responses.
- **Geographic availability:** Is the model served from regions that meet our latency and compliance requirements?
- **Rate limits:** Are there limits on how many requests we can make per minute or per day, and do they meet our peak traffic needs?

---

### Criterion 5: Cost
**What it means:** What will it cost to operate this model at our expected usage volume, and does the pricing model align with our usage pattern?

Most LLM APIs are priced per token — we pay for the volume of text we send in (input tokens) and receive back (output tokens). Costs vary substantially between models:

- **High-capability models** (Gemini 2.5 Pro): Higher cost per token, appropriate for complex tasks where quality is paramount
- **Fast, efficient models** (Gemini 2.0 Flash): Lower cost per token, appropriate for high-volume, lower-complexity tasks
- **Open-weight models** (Gemma): Model weights are free; we pay only for the compute to run them on our own infrastructure

**Business example:** A legal research tool used by a small team of analysts may tolerate the higher cost of a top-tier model because the quality improvement justifies it. A customer-facing chatbot answering thousands of simple questions per day benefits from a cost-efficient model that keeps per-interaction costs minimal.

---

### Criterion 6: Performance
**What it means:** How well does the model perform on the specific task we need — both in terms of output quality and speed?

Performance is task-specific. A model that scores highest on general benchmarks may not be the best choice for our particular use case. Practical evaluation — testing candidate models on a representative sample of our actual use cases — is essential.

Key dimensions:
- **Accuracy:** Does the model give correct, relevant answers on our specific tasks?
- **Instruction following:** Does the model reliably do what we ask, in the format we requested?
- **Speed (latency):** How quickly does it respond?
- **Consistency:** Does it produce similar quality outputs reliably, or does quality vary widely?

---

### Criterion 7: Fine-Tuning Support
**What it means:** Can the model be further trained on our domain-specific data to improve performance on our use case?

Fine-tuning is not always necessary — many use cases are well-served by prompting alone or RAG. But when we need the model to internalize specific knowledge, terminology, formatting conventions, or behavioral styles that are unique to our organization, fine-tuning may be necessary.

Questions to ask:
- Does the provider offer fine-tuning API access for this model?
- What is the cost of fine-tuning (typically charged per training token)?
- What data volume is needed for effective fine-tuning?
- Does fine-tuning update the shared model or create a private model copy? (Private copies are the enterprise standard.)

---

### Criterion 8: Customization Options
**What it means:** What techniques are available to adapt the model for our specific needs, and how do they compare in effort, cost, and effectiveness?

The customization spectrum, from lightest to most intensive:

| Technique | Effort | Cost | When to Use |
|-----------|--------|------|-------------|
| **Prompting** | Very low | No extra cost | Most use cases; always try this first |
| **Prompt tuning** | Low | Low | When prompting alone doesn't achieve target quality |
| **RAG** | Medium | Storage + retrieval compute | When we need the model to be knowledgeable about our specific data |
| **Fine-tuning** | High | Training compute + data | When prompting + RAG can't achieve needed performance; style/tone consistency |
| **Full pre-training** | Extremely high | Massive compute + data | Almost never — reserved for organizations building their own foundation models |

## Foundation Model Selection Framework
Use this table to map our requirements to the most relevant selection criteria:

| Our Requirement | Most Critical Criteria |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| Analyzing documents with charts, tables, and images | Modality (multimodal), Context window |
| Customer-facing real-time chat | Performance (latency), Availability, Cost |
| Sensitive or regulated data processing | Security/compliance, consider Gemma (on-premises) |
| High-volume, cost-sensitive automation | Cost, Performance |
| Specialized domain (legal, medical, financial) | Fine-tuning support, Performance on domain tasks |
| Enterprise integration with Google Workspace | Gemini + Google ecosystem integration |
| Full data control, on-premises deployment | Open-weight models (Gemma), Modality |
| Video or image content generation | Modality (Veo, Imagen) |

The most common mistake organizations make is choosing the most capable (and most expensive) model for every use case. A tiered model strategy — using high-capability models for complex tasks, efficient models for routine tasks, and open-weight models for sensitive data — is typically both more cost-effective and more appropriate.
