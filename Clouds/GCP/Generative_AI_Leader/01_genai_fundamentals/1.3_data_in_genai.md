# 1.3 Data in Generative AI

Data is the raw material of artificial intelligence. A sophisticated AI model applied to poor-quality data produces poor-quality results. An organization with remarkable proprietary data and a well-designed AI strategy can create competitive advantages that are difficult to replicate.

This document covers the dimensions of data quality, the fundamental distinction between structured and unstructured data, the difference between labeled and unlabeled data, and how each of these concepts applies to practical GenAI deployments.

---

## Why Data Quality Matters More Than Model Sophistication
A common misconception about AI is that a sufficiently powerful model can compensate for poor data. In practice, the opposite is closer to the truth: a simpler model trained on excellent data frequently outperforms a complex model trained on mediocre data.

For organizations considering AI investments, data quality assessment should come before model selection. If the data foundation is weak, the investment in AI will underperform expectations — and the root cause will be misdiagnosed as a model problem or an AI technology limitation, when it is actually a data problem.

### Data Quality
The internal health and truthfulness of our data determines the reliability of our AI's outputs. Five key dimensions define data quality:

---

#### Accuracy
**Definition:** Data values are factually correct, precise, and mirror real-world truths without errors.

**What inaccurate data looks like:** Customer support wikis containing deprecated product specifications, or financial logs with transposed numbers.

**GenAI Implications:** GenAI models treat our provided data as absolute truth. If an internal knowledge base contains inaccurate information, a RAG system will confidently surface those falsehoods to users, resulting in critical misinformation or severe hallucinations.

---

#### Completeness
**Definition:** Complete data has all the values and records necessary for the intended use — no gaps, missing fields, or absent records.

**What incomplete data looks like:**
- Customer profiles missing purchase history for a subset of customers
- Medical records where diagnostic codes are present but treatment outcomes are absent
- Sensor data with regular gaps from equipment outages
- Employee records where certain fields were optional and frequently left blank

**Business implications for AI:**
- A model cannot learn from what it cannot see. If 30% of our customer records are missing the feature most predictive of churn, our churn prediction model is working with one hand tied behind its back.
- For generative AI applications, incomplete knowledge bases produce responses with gaps — the AI will either hallucinate to fill them or admit it doesn't know, depending on its configuration.
- Missing data can introduce systematic bias: if the customers whose data is missing are disproportionately from a particular segment, the model may perform worse for that segment.

**What to do:** Audit data completeness before building models. For important fields, estimate the missingness rate and assess whether it is random or systematic. Consider data collection improvements or alternative data sources for critical gaps.

---

#### Representative
**Definition:** The dataset accurately reflects the entire operational reality, population diversity, and variety of scenarios the AI will encounter in production.

**What unrepresentative data looks like:** Training a customer service agent entirely on technical support logs from enterprise IT clients, then deploying it to handle non-technical retail consumers.

**GenAI Implications:** Skewed or narrow data causes severe behavioral bias. If our historical data fails to represent specific user demographics, edge-case problems, or regional dialects, the live generative model will perform poorly or fail entirely when interacting with those unrepresented groups.

---

#### Consistency
**Definition:** Consistent data uses the same formats, definitions, and values across records and systems. The same underlying concept is represented the same way everywhere.

**What inconsistent data looks like:**
- Customer addresses stored differently across systems: "123 Main St," "123 Main Street," "123 main st."
- Product categories named differently in the sales system and the inventory system
- Dates stored in MM/DD/YYYY in one system and YYYY-MM-DD in another
- The same customer appearing under five different IDs because records were never deduplicated

**Business implications for AI:**
- Models learn from patterns. Inconsistent representations of the same concept confuse the model and degrade performance.
- For generative AI knowledge bases, inconsistent information produces inconsistent answers — the model may give different answers to the same question depending on which document it retrieved.
- In RAG applications, inconsistent terminology between user queries and stored documents means relevant content may not be retrieved.

**What to do:** Data quality rules and validation pipelines help catch inconsistencies at ingestion. For historical data, deduplication and normalization may be needed before AI use.

---

#### Relevance
**Definition:** Relevant data is appropriate and pertinent to the task at hand — it contains the information the model needs, and is not diluted with irrelevant content.

**What irrelevant data looks like:**
- Training a customer service response model on generic internet content rather than our company's actual customer interactions
- Including every field from a database in a model that only needs three of them, introducing noise
- Using data from a very different industry or time period for a domain-specific application
- Including boilerplate legal language in every document when the model is supposed to learn content-specific patterns

**Business implications for AI:**
- Irrelevant data dilutes the model's learning signal — it has to "waste" capacity on patterns that don't help it accomplish the task.
- For fine-tuning, highly relevant, curated data produces better results than large amounts of loosely related data.
- For RAG knowledge bases, including irrelevant documents increases the chance of retrieving the wrong content and degrades response quality.

**What to do:** Be deliberate about what data goes into training or knowledge base pipelines. More data is not always better — more *relevant* data is better.

---

### Data Accessibility
Data accessibility dictates whether an AI workflow can legally, financially, and technically ingest data at scale.

---

#### Availability
**Definition:** Available data is data that can actually be accessed for AI use — it is not siloed, locked behind access restrictions, or unusable due to technical or legal barriers.

**What unavailability looks like:**
- Data exists in a system that lacks an API or export capability
- Data is technically accessible but subject to contracts that prohibit AI training use
- Data is held by third parties who won't share it or charge prohibitively for access
- Data is in formats (physical paper records, legacy formats) that require manual conversion
- Data governance rules require individual consent that hasn't been obtained

**Business implications for AI:**
- The most valuable data for our AI use case may be the least available. Customer interaction data may be the most relevant for our support AI, but call recordings may require consent management before they can be used.
- Regulatory constraints (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA, sector-specific regulations) limit what personal data can be used for model training.
- Data availability assessment is a critical part of AI feasibility analysis — discovering midway through a project that key data can't be used causes expensive delays.

**What to do:** Conduct a data availability audit early in any AI initiative. Map the data we ideally need against what we can legally and practically access. Plan data governance structures proactively.

---

#### Cost
**Definition:** Data cost includes all the resources required to acquire, label, store, process, and maintain data for AI use.

**Components of data cost:**
- **Acquisition:** Licensing third-party data, purchasing datasets, incentivizing users to provide data
- **Labeling:** Paying humans to annotate training data — can cost thousands to millions of dollars for large labeled datasets
- **Storage:** Cloud storage costs for large datasets, especially images, audio, and video
- **Processing:** Compute costs for data pipelines, cleaning, transformation
- **Maintenance:** Ongoing costs to keep data current, remove stale records, maintain data quality

**Business implications for AI:**
- Data labeling is often the biggest hidden cost in ML projects. Labeling 100,000 images for an object detection model can cost tens of thousands of dollars and take months.
- This cost reality is part of why foundation models matter: they were trained on enormous datasets so organizations don't have to reproduce that investment. Most organizations are adapting rather than building from scratch.
- For fine-tuning, even a few thousand high-quality labeled examples can be valuable — but acquiring those examples has real cost.

**What to do:** Budget data costs explicitly. Use active learning techniques (have the model identify which examples would be most valuable to label) to reduce labeling cost. Leverage foundation models + RAG as an alternative to fine-tuning when possible.

---

#### Format
**Definition:** Data format refers to how information is encoded, structured, and stored. For AI use, data must be in a format the model can ingest — or must be converted to that format.

**Format considerations:**
- **File types:** PDFs, Word documents, images (JPEG, PNG, TIFF), audio (MP3, WAV), video (MP4), spreadsheets (Excel, CSV), databases (SQL, NoSQL)
- **Encoding:** Character encoding, compression, proprietary formats
- **Structure:** Whether the data has consistent schema or varies record by record
- **Quality:** For images and audio, resolution and recording quality affect model performance

**Business implications for AI:**
- Scanned PDFs (images of text) require OCR processing before text can be extracted; the quality of the OCR affects downstream model performance
- Legacy data in proprietary formats (old database exports, decade-old file formats) may require significant conversion effort
- Audio quality matters: a speech recognition or analysis model will perform better on clean recordings than on low-quality call center recordings

**What to do:** Inventory our data formats early. Identify conversion requirements and associated costs. For new data collection, design for AI-friendliness from the start.

## Structured vs. Unstructured Data
One of the most important distinctions in enterprise data is between structured, unstructured, and semi-structured data. Each type has different properties, different storage requirements, and different roles in AI systems.

---

### Structured Data
**Definition:** Structured data is organized into a predefined format with a consistent schema — rows and columns, with each field having a defined type and meaning.

**Real-world examples:**
- A database table of customer records: CustomerID, Name, Email, Registration Date, Total Purchases
- A spreadsheet tracking employee performance: Employee ID, Department, Quarter, Performance Score, Manager Rating
- Financial transaction records: Transaction ID, Date, Amount, Account Number, Merchant Category
- Inventory data: SKU, Product Name, Quantity in Stock, Warehouse Location, Reorder Point
- Healthcare records in a structured database: Patient ID, Diagnosis Code, Procedure Code, Date of Service

**Characteristics:**
- Easily queryable with SQL or similar tools
- High information density (lots of data per unit of storage)
- Relationships between records can be explicitly defined
- Traditional business intelligence and analytics tools work well with it
- Can be directly fed into many traditional ML models as numerical features

**AI applications:** Structured data is the backbone of traditional ML — fraud detection models, churn prediction models, demand forecasting, credit scoring. These models ingest structured features and produce structured outputs (probabilities, scores, classifications).

---

### Unstructured Data
**Definition:** Unstructured data has no predefined schema or consistent format — it is free-form content that doesn't fit neatly into rows and columns.

**Real-world examples:**
- Emails and email threads
- Word documents, PDFs, PowerPoint presentations
- Customer service call recordings
- Product reviews and social media posts
- Photographs, medical images, satellite imagery
- Video recordings (security footage, training videos, product demonstrations)
- Web pages and blog posts
- Handwritten notes and forms

**Characteristics:**
- Cannot be queried directly with SQL
- Requires specialized processing to extract meaning (NLP for text, computer vision for images, speech recognition for audio)
- Often contains the richest, most nuanced information about human behavior and intent
- Difficult to store and search at scale without specialized infrastructure
- Represents approximately 80% of all enterprise data

**Why unstructured data matters so much for GenAI:** Large language models were designed precisely to work with unstructured text. The text a model was trained on — books, articles, documentation, code, conversations — is all unstructured. When an organization connects a GenAI model to its unstructured data (internal documents, emails, support transcripts, wikis), it unlocks the value in data that was previously inaccessible to traditional analytics.

**AI applications:** Text analysis, document understanding, content generation, customer sentiment analysis, knowledge base construction for RAG, image captioning, video summarization.

---

### Semi-Structured Data
**Definition:** Semi-structured data has some organizational properties — like tags, labels, or markers — but doesn't conform to a rigid relational schema. It lies between fully structured and fully unstructured.

**Real-world examples:**
- **JSON:** `{"customer_id": "12345", "name": "Priya Sharma", "preferences": ["electronics", "books"]}`
- **XML:** HTML, configuration files, data exchange formats
- **Log files:** Timestamped entries with consistent patterns but variable content
- **Email metadata:** Headers have a defined structure; the body is unstructured
- **CSV files with inconsistent fields:** Some structure, but not a rigid relational schema

**Characteristics:**
- Flexible schema — fields may vary between records
- Human-readable formats (JSON, XML) are also machine-processable
- Often generated by applications, APIs, and system logs
- Increasingly common as the default format for modern data exchange

---

### Comparison Table

| Property | Structured Data | Semi-Structured Data | Unstructured Data |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Schema** | Rigid, predefined | Flexible/self-describing | None |
| **Examples** | Databases, spreadsheets | JSON, XML, logs | Text, images, audio, video |
| **Query method** | SQL | Document queries (NoSQL) | AI/ML, full-text search |
| **Storage** | Relational databases, data warehouses | Document stores, object storage | Object storage, specialized indexes |
| **Approximate % of enterprise data** | ~20% | ~10% | ~70–80% |
| **Traditional analytics** | Easy | Moderate | Difficult |
| **GenAI suitability** | Good (as context/features) | Good | Excellent |

---

### Business Implications of Each Type

**Structured data for AI:**
- Powers traditional ML for prediction and classification tasks
- Clean and well-organized, making it easy to use for model training
- Valuable as context in GenAI applications (a customer's purchase history, provided as structured context to a recommendations chatbot)
- Limited in capturing nuance, intent, and complex human communication

**Unstructured data for GenAI:**
- The primary input type for large language models
- Most enterprise unstructured data was previously "dark data" — stored but not analyzed at scale
- GenAI makes this data newly accessible: we can now ask questions of our document library, extract structured information from contracts, summarize call recordings
- Organizations with large, rich archives of domain-specific unstructured data (legal precedents, medical literature, engineering documentation) have a competitive advantage if they can apply AI effectively

**Semi-structured data for AI:**
- Increasingly the interface between enterprise systems (APIs exchange JSON)
- Log file analysis with AI can find patterns in system behavior
- Email metadata combined with email content (unstructured) enables richer analysis than either alone

## Labeled vs. Unlabeled Data
This distinction is central to understanding how different AI systems are trained and why foundation models represent such a change in what's possible.

---

### Labeled Data
**Definition:** Labeled data is a dataset where each example has been annotated with the correct answer — a human (or automated system) has tagged each item with the category, value, or attribute the model is supposed to learn.

**Examples of labeled data:**
- An email dataset where each message is tagged "spam" or "not spam" — 10,000 spam emails and 10,000 legitimate emails, each labeled
- A collection of product reviews where each review is annotated with a sentiment score: 1 (very negative) to 5 (very positive)
- Images of manufacturing parts, each labeled as "defect" or "no defect" with the defect location marked
- Audio recordings of customer service calls, each labeled by outcome: "resolved," "escalated," "abandoned"
- Medical X-rays, each annotated by a radiologist indicating which diagnoses are present

**Why you need labeled data:**
- **Supervised learning** requires labeled examples to learn from — the label is the "supervision" that tells the model when it's right and wrong
- **Fine-tuning** foundation models for specific tasks typically requires labeled examples of the desired input-output pairs
- **Evaluation** of any model requires labeled test data to measure performance against ground truth

**The cost and effort of labeling:**
Labeling is expensive, time-consuming, and often requires domain expertise. Consider what is involved in labeling a dataset for medical image analysis: each image must be reviewed by a qualified radiologist, who annotates findings. At medical radiologist billing rates, labeling 10,000 X-rays could cost millions of dollars. Even simpler labeling tasks — classifying customer service transcripts into resolution categories — require significant human time and judgment.

Common labeling approaches:
- **Internal subject matter experts:** High quality but expensive (their time is valuable)
- **Crowdsourced labeling:** More scalable and cheaper, but requires quality control processes
- **Programmatic labeling:** Using rules or weaker models to generate labels automatically (faster and cheaper but lower quality)
- **Active learning:** Having the model identify which unlabeled examples it is least certain about and prioritizing those for human labeling — maximizes information gained per labeling dollar spent

---

### Unlabeled Data
**Definition:** Unlabeled data is raw data without any annotations — just the examples themselves, with no ground-truth answers provided.

**Examples of unlabeled data:**
- A collection of web pages crawled from the internet — just the text content, no labels
- A year's worth of internal employee emails — just the messages, no categorization
- Millions of images from a product photography database — just the images, no descriptions
- Call center audio recordings — just the audio, no transcripts or outcome labels

**When unlabeled data is used:**
- **Foundation model pre-training:** This is the key insight. Foundation models like Gemini are trained on massive amounts of unlabeled text. The training signal comes not from human-applied labels, but from the structure of the data itself — predicting the next word in a sentence is a self-supervised objective that requires no external labels. The model gets "supervised" by the data itself.
- **Unsupervised learning:** Clustering, anomaly detection, and representation learning can all operate on unlabeled data
- **Augmentation:** Unlabeled data from the same domain can improve model performance even when combined with limited labeled data

---

### How Foundation Models Changed the Equation
Before foundation models, the standard recipe for building a text classification or language understanding system was:
1. Collect thousands of domain-specific labeled examples
2. Train a model from scratch (or fine-tune a smaller pre-trained model) on those labeled examples
3. Deploy — a model that works specifically for that task and domain

The bottleneck was always labeled data. Each new task required new labeled data. Each new domain required domain-specific annotations.

Foundation models changed this in three ways:

**1. Pre-training on unlabeled data at scale:** A foundation model like Gemini was trained on hundreds of billions of words of text without any human-applied labels. The scale of pre-training gives it broad language understanding and world knowledge before we write a single line of application code.

**2. Few-shot and zero-shot learning:** Because foundation models have learned so much from pre-training, they can often perform new tasks with few or no task-specific examples. Show a foundation model a few examples of what we want (few-shot) or just describe the task in plain language (zero-shot), and it can often perform it well.

**3. Reducing labeled data requirements:** When fine-tuning is needed, foundation models require far less labeled data than training from scratch. A few hundred to a few thousand examples of our domain-specific task may be enough to fine-tune a foundation model to strong performance — compared to the tens of thousands needed for earlier approaches.

**Business implication:** The high cost of data labeling, which previously created a significant barrier to building AI capabilities, is dramatically reduced for many use cases. Organizations can now build useful AI applications even when their labeled data is limited, by leveraging the pre-training that foundation model providers have already invested in.

## How Data Types Are Used in GenAI

### Text Data in LLMs
Large language models are trained on text and generate text. The text data they learn from includes:
- Books, academic papers, and articles (breadth of knowledge)
- Web pages (broad coverage of topics, styles, and formats)
- Code repositories (enabling code generation)
- Conversational data (enabling natural dialogue)

For enterprise RAG applications, the organization's proprietary text — policies, documentation, contracts, email archives, knowledge bases — becomes the specialized knowledge layer that makes a general model knowledgeable about our specific domain.

### Image Data in Vision Models
Image generation and understanding models like Imagen and the vision capabilities of Gemini are trained on large collections of images, often paired with text descriptions. The model learns the visual patterns of the world well enough to recognize objects in new images and generate images matching textual descriptions.

For business applications, domain-specific image data matters: a model used for detecting manufacturing defects performs better when fine-tuned on images of our specific products and defect types.

### Code Data in Code Generation Models
Models trained on large code repositories — GitHub, public package repositories, technical documentation — learn the syntax, patterns, idioms, and best practices of programming languages. This enables them to generate syntactically correct, logically sound code, explain existing code, and find bugs.

CodeGemma and the code generation capabilities of Gemini were trained with significant attention to code quality and coverage across languages.

### Multimodal Data Combinations
The most capable models are trained on combinations of data types simultaneously:
- Text paired with images (image-text pairs for visual understanding)
- Audio paired with transcriptions (speech recognition training)
- Video paired with descriptions (video understanding)
- Code paired with natural language descriptions (code generation and explanation)

The interleaving of these modalities during training is what enables genuinely multimodal reasoning — not just applying separate models to separate content types, but understanding relationships between modalities.

## Proprietary Data as a Competitive Asset
One of the most strategically significant implications of the foundation model era is the role of proprietary data.

In a world where every organization has access to the same foundation models, the differentiator is not the model itself — it is the unique data and domain knowledge that can be used to customize and ground the model.

Consider two financial services firms deploying the same Gemini model for investment research. The firm that can connect the model to a decades-long archive of proprietary deal flow data, unique market research, and annotated analyst decisions will produce a more differentiated, valuable application than the firm that connects the same model to generic public information.

**Strategic implications:**
- Organizations should actively audit and value their proprietary data assets — what information does our organization have that no one else has?
- Data collection strategies should be designed with future AI use in mind — are the interactions we are having with customers being captured in reusable form?
- Data moats — unique, difficult-to-replicate data assets — are a sustainable competitive advantage in the AI era in a way that model access is not

The organizations that will benefit most from AI are those that combine world-class foundation models with deep, rich, proprietary data — and have the operational discipline to keep that data high quality over time.
