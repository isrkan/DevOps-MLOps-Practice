# 3.3 Grounding, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, and Sampling Parameters

One of the most practical and widely deployed techniques for improving AI output is **grounding** — and understanding it well gives us the conceptual foundation for understanding a large family of AI improvement strategies.

**What grounding is:** Grounding is the practice of connecting a model's responses to specific, verified information sources rather than relying solely on what the model learned during training. A grounded model does not answer purely from memory — it consults designated sources before or during response generation, and anchors its answer in what it finds there.

**Why grounding matters:**
- It **reduces hallucination** — the model has actual content to reference instead of generating freely from statistical patterns
- It **overcomes the knowledge cutoff** — current sources can be grounded in real time, regardless of the model's training date
- It **enables proprietary knowledge** — our internal documents, policies, and data become accessible to the model without retraining
- It **makes responses verifiable** — when a grounded model cites a source, that source can be checked, audited, and trusted
- It **builds user trust** — responses backed by visible citations are more credible than responses with no traceable basis

---

## Types of Grounding by Data Source
Not all grounded knowledge is the same. Grounding can connect a model to very different types of information sources, each with different properties and use cases.

### Grounding with First-Party Enterprise Data
**What it is:** Connecting the AI to our organization's own internal data — the documents, databases, knowledge bases, policies, product catalogs, training materials, and procedural guides that our company has created and owns.

**Why it is strategically important:** Our internal data is our competitive advantage. A model that knows our specific products, processes, customers, and organizational context is far more useful than a generic model. This is the knowledge that differentiates our AI from any competitor using the same underlying foundation model.

**How it works:** Our internal documents are processed and indexed in a searchable system. When a question is asked, the system searches our indexed content for the most relevant material and provides it to the model as context. The model generates a response grounded in our specific documents.

**Real-world examples:**
- A **customer service AI** grounded in our support knowledge base, product documentation, and customer-facing policies. When a customer asks about return eligibility, the AI retrieves our actual return policy and answers from it — not from a generic understanding of how return policies typically work.
- An **HR AI assistant** grounded in our employee handbook, benefits documentation, and HR policies. When an employee asks about parental leave eligibility, the AI references our specific policy, not a generic description of how parental leave often works.
- A **sales assistant** grounded in our product catalog, pricing documentation, and competitive battlecards. When a salesperson needs to address a specific customer objection, the AI retrieves the relevant competitive talking points from our actual sales materials.
- An **internal knowledge base assistant** grounded in our company's engineering documentation, process guides, and institutional knowledge. New employees can ask questions and get answers based on our organization's specific practices.

**Privacy and security consideration:** Our internal data stays within our enterprise environment under our control. It is not shared with external parties, does not become part of any model's training data (unless we explicitly choose that), and can be access-controlled so that different users can only see the internal documents they are authorized to access.

---

### Grounding with Third-Party Data
**What it is:** Connecting the AI to curated, authoritative external data sources that our organization has licensed or integrated — industry databases, regulatory repositories, market data providers, legal databases, technical standards, or other specialized external knowledge.

**Why it is valuable:** For domains where external authoritative sources are more comprehensive, current, or credible than anything our organization could maintain internally, third-party grounding provides the AI with expert-level domain knowledge.

**Real-world examples:**
- A **legal research assistant** grounded in legal databases such as case law repositories, statutory databases, and regulatory publications. When an attorney asks about case precedent, the AI retrieves actual legal citations from authoritative sources.
- A **financial analysis tool** grounded in a licensed market data provider. When an analyst asks about current equity valuations or recent earnings reports, the AI references live or current market data.
- A **compliance monitoring assistant** grounded in regulatory publications and compliance databases for our specific industry. As regulations update, the grounded knowledge base updates, and the AI's responses reflect current requirements.
- A **technical support tool** grounded in licensed technology partner documentation, technical standards bodies, or vendor knowledge bases that are not publicly accessible.

**Business consideration:** Third-party grounding requires appropriate licensing of the data source. We need the legal right to use the content in our AI system — not just to read it as a human would. Organizations should ensure their AI data licensing agreements explicitly cover use as grounding context.

---

### Grounding with World Data (Google Search)
**What it is:** Connecting the AI to live web search results — real-time, broad public information about the world. When the model is grounded with Google Search, it retrieves current web content relevant to the query before generating a response.

**Why it is useful:** This directly overcomes the knowledge cutoff limitation. A model grounded with Google Search does not have to rely on what it knew at training time — it can retrieve what is publicly known right now.

**When to use it:**
- When responses need to reflect current events, recent news, or recent developments
- When answering questions about organizations, products, or public figures whose situations may have changed since training
- When fact-checking against publicly available information
- When we need broad world knowledge currency, not specialized organizational depth

**Business examples:**
- A **market intelligence assistant** that tracks competitor announcements and industry news. When a product manager asks "What has our main competitor announced in the last 30 days?", the assistant retrieves and synthesizes recent public announcements.
- A **procurement research tool** that helps buyers understand current market conditions, recent supplier news, or pricing trends — grounded in current web content rather than training-time assumptions.
- A **press and communications tool** that needs to incorporate current events and recent company announcements from public sources.

**Important nuance:** Grounding with Google Search is best for broad, publicly available information where currency is important. For our organization's specific internal knowledge, first-party data grounding remains essential — public search will not have our internal policies, our proprietary processes, or our confidential business information.

**Google's offering:** Google Cloud offers **Grounding with Google Search** as a built-in capability for Agent Platform models. Enabling it connects our Agent Platform model to Google Search in real time, with automatic retrieval at query time.

## Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
RAG is the most widely deployed and technically important grounding architecture. Understanding RAG well gives us a clear mental model of how AI systems access organizational knowledge.

### What RAG Is
RAG stands for **Retrieval-Augmented Generation** — a name that describes exactly what it does. It combines a **retrieval** step (finding relevant content from a knowledge base) with the **generation** step (the model producing a response), augmenting what the model knows from training with what it retrieves from our knowledge sources.

The key innovation of RAG is using **semantic similarity** for retrieval. Traditional search systems find documents that contain the same words we searched for. RAG finds documents that are semantically similar — documents about the same concept, even if they use different words. This is far more useful in practice.

If a user asks "What is the policy for employees working from home when visiting a client city?" and our document calls it "remote work during business travel," a keyword search might miss it. A semantic search finds it because the meaning is similar, even though the words are different.

### How RAG Works — Step by Step
Here is what happens from the moment a user asks a question to the moment they receive an answer in a RAG system:

**Step 1: The user asks a question** - A user types a question: "What is our policy on remote work expenses for employees traveling to client sites?"

**Step 2: The question is converted to a vector embedding** - The question is passed through an **embedding model** — a specialized AI model that converts text into a numerical vector (essentially a long list of numbers) that represents the meaning of the question mathematically. Two pieces of text with similar meanings produce vectors that are close to each other in mathematical space; texts with different meanings produce vectors that are far apart. Think of it as translating the meaning of the question into coordinates in a conceptual space, where proximity means similarity of meaning.

**Step 3: The vector database is searched** - Our organization's documents have previously been processed through the same embedding model and stored as vectors in a **vector database** — a database optimized for finding similar vectors quickly. The question's vector is compared against all the document vectors in the database to find the documents that are mathematically closest in meaning — the most semantically relevant documents.

**Step 4: The most relevant document sections are retrieved** - The top matching documents (or document sections, called "chunks") are retrieved from the vector database. Typically, three to ten chunks are retrieved, depending on how the system is configured.

**Step 5: The retrieved content is added to the prompt** - The retrieved document chunks are inserted into the prompt that is sent to the language model. The full prompt now includes: the original question, the retrieved document content, and instructions telling the model to answer based on the provided content.

**Step 6: The model generates a grounded response** - The language model reads the full prompt — including the retrieved documents — and generates a response based on that content. Because the relevant policy document is in the context, the model answers from the actual policy rather than from its general training knowledge.

**The result:** The user receives an accurate answer based on our organization's actual documented policy, with the ability to trace which document was used.

### Why RAG Works Well for Enterprise Knowledge
RAG aligns naturally with how organizational knowledge management actually works:
- **Knowledge changes frequently.** Policies update, products are revised, procedures change. RAG knowledge bases can be updated without retraining the model. Update the documents, re-index them, and the model immediately has access to the new content.
- **Knowledge is spread across many documents.** No single document contains everything. RAG retrieves the most relevant sections from across our entire knowledge base for each individual query.
- **Answers should be traceable.** RAG can be configured to cite which documents were retrieved for each response, providing an audit trail that purely generative responses lack.
- **Cost efficiency.** Training a model is expensive; updating a document database is not. RAG gives us much of the knowledge benefit of fine-tuning at a fraction of the cost and time.

### RAG Compared to Fine-Tuning
Both RAG and fine-tuning address the lack of organizational knowledge in a foundation model — but in fundamentally different ways:

| Dimension | RAG | Fine-Tuning |
|-----------|-----|-------------|
| How it works | Retrieves relevant content at query time | Trains the model weights on domain data |
| Knowledge update | Update the database — immediate effect | Requires retraining — time and cost |
| Cost | Low-medium (indexing + retrieval) | High (compute, data preparation, engineering) |
| Traceability | Can cite source documents | Cannot directly cite training examples |
| Best for | Current, document-based organizational knowledge | Deep domain specialization, style consistency |
| Starting recommendation | Yes — start here | Only after RAG has been tried and found insufficient |

- Customer support chatbots grounded in product documentation and support knowledge bases

- Product search and discovery applications
- Organizations without deep ML engineering teams who need fast, reliable deployment

### RAG APIs (Agent Platform RAG Engine)
**What it is:** The Agent Platform RAG Engine exposes lower-level APIs that give developers precise control over each component of the RAG pipeline.

**What you can control:**
- **Document chunking strategy** — how documents are broken into retrievable segments (by sentence, paragraph, fixed character count, semantically meaningful sections)
- **Embedding model selection** — which model converts documents and queries into vectors
- **Retrieval parameters** — how many document chunks to retrieve, similarity threshold settings
- **Reranking** — a second pass that re-orders retrieved results for better relevance before passing them to the LLM
- **Hybrid search** — combining semantic (vector) search with keyword search for better recall on both meaning and specific terms

**Effort level:** Higher. Requires engineering work to configure, tune, and maintain the pipeline.

**Best for:**
- Organizations with specialized requirements the pre-built solution does not cover
- Legal, financial, or medical applications where specific citation formats or retrieval precision requirements apply
- Documents with unusual structures that require custom chunking logic
- High-volume deployments where retrieval performance needs careful tuning
- Organizations building proprietary RAG infrastructure as a competitive differentiator

## Sampling Parameters — Controlling How the Model Generates Output
Beyond what information the model has access to, how it generates its output is controlled by a set of parameters called **sampling parameters** (also called **decoding parameters**). These settings affect the mechanics of how the model picks the words in its response — how creative or consistent it is, how long it writes, and what it will and will not say.

Understanding these parameters allows us to tune model behavior for our specific use case, rather than accepting whatever defaults a vendor has configured — defaults that are designed for average use cases, not our specific one.

---

### Temperature
**What it controls:** Temperature is the most important and widely understood sampling parameter. It controls the trade-off between predictability and creativity in the model's outputs.

**How it works — the intuition:** At each step of generating text, the model considers many possible next words, each with a different probability. Temperature determines how the model chooses between them.

At **low temperature (near 0):** The model heavily favors the highest-probability next word at every step. The result is consistent, predictable output that closely follows the most statistically likely path. Each time we send the same prompt, we get nearly the same response. The model "plays it safe."

At **high temperature (1.0+):** The model gives meaningful weight to lower-probability alternatives, making it more likely to choose unexpected or creative words. The result is varied, creative, sometimes surprising output. Responses to the same prompt vary more. The model "takes more chances."

**Temperature settings in practice:**

| Temperature | Behavior | When to Use |
|-------------|----------|-------------|
| 0.0 | Maximally deterministic — nearly identical responses every time | Factual extraction, data processing, precise coding tasks |
| 0.1–0.3 | Very consistent, careful, accurate | Customer service Q&A, factual responses, compliance content |
| 0.4–0.6 | Balanced — consistent with natural variation | General business writing, summarization, analysis |
| 0.7–0.9 | Creative and varied | Marketing copy, content generation, multiple draft options |
| 1.0+ | Highly creative, unpredictable | Creative writing exploration, diverse ideation, brainstorming |

**Business guidance:**
- For a **customer service application**, use low temperature (0.1–0.3). When two customers ask the same question, they should receive the same accurate, consistent answer. Unpredictable variation is a problem, not a feature. High temperature in a customer service context means different customers get different information about the same policy — a reliability and trust issue.
- For a **content generation tool** used by a marketing team, moderate to higher temperature (0.6–0.9) is better. Writers want varied, creative suggestions — they will select and refine the best ones. If every draft is nearly the same, the tool provides little value.
- For a **data extraction or classification task**, use very low temperature (0.0–0.1). Precision, consistency, and reproducibility matter. We do not want the AI to classify the same record differently depending on which way the random dice rolled.

**What leaders need to understand:** Most AI products have a default temperature configured by the vendor. If we are deploying an AI system or evaluating a vendor's product, ask what temperature is set and verify it is appropriate for our use case. A customer service bot misconfigured at high temperature will produce variably creative (and potentially inconsistent or incorrect) responses when we need reliable ones.

---

### Top-P (Nucleus Sampling)
**What it controls:** Top-P, also called nucleus sampling, controls which possible next words the model considers at each step of generation. It defines the vocabulary the model can choose from at any given moment.

**How it works:** Instead of considering every possible next word (the full vocabulary), the model considers only the smallest set of words whose cumulative probability adds up to the threshold P.

- At **Top-P = 0.9:** The model considers the words that together account for 90% of the probability mass — the most likely candidates — and ignores everything that falls in the low-probability tail.
- At **Top-P = 0.5:** Even more restricted — only the words accounting for the top 50% of probability are considered.
- At **Top-P = 1.0:** No filtering — all words are in consideration.

**The effect:** Top-P prevents the model from selecting very unlikely words (which are often poor choices, incoherent, or random-seeming) while still allowing meaningful variety among the plausible candidates.

**Relationship to temperature:** Both temperature and Top-P affect output variety. Temperature shapes how the probability distribution is used; Top-P trims what is in the distribution in the first place. They work in combination:
- A common production configuration: temperature = 0.2–0.4 with Top-P = 0.9 — the temperature keeps responses consistent and careful, while Top-P ensures a reasonable vocabulary range is available
- For creative tasks: temperature = 0.7–0.9 with Top-P = 0.95 — more variety in both temperature and vocabulary selection

**Business guidance:** For most standard business applications, a Top-P of 0.85–0.95 combined with an appropriate temperature produces good results without requiring deep tuning. Very low Top-P (below 0.5) can make responses feel repetitive and formulaic; Top-P of 1.0 allows occasional very unlikely word choices that can appear odd.

### Token Count and Maximum Output Length
**What it controls:** The maximum number of tokens the model will generate in its response. This is a hard cap — once the model reaches this limit, it stops generating, even if the response would naturally continue further.

**Why it matters:**

**Cost management.** Most AI providers charge per token — both input tokens in our prompt and output tokens in the response. Without a maximum output length limit, a prompt that somehow triggers a very long response generates proportionally higher costs. For production deployments processing thousands of requests per day, this matters significantly.

**User experience.** Excessively long responses are not always better responses. For a chat interface, a paragraph is often more useful than a five-page essay. For a dashboard widget, a single sentence is ideal. Constraining output length shapes the model toward producing responses that fit the interface and the user's needs.

**Application fit.** Some applications have technical constraints — a response that must fit in a UI element, a reply that must stay within an API field's character limit, or a response formatted for SMS or push notification delivery.

**Guidance by use case:**

| Use Case | Typical Max Tokens | Rationale |
|----------|--------------------|-----------|
| Chat responses | 150–400 | Conversational, fits in the chat window |
| Summaries | 200–500 | Concise but complete |
| Detailed analysis | 800–2,000 | Depth is expected and valuable |
| Full document drafts | 1,500–4,000+ | Full document production |
| Code generation | Variable (often 500–2,000+) | Code completeness matters |
| Data extraction / classification | 50–200 | Structured, minimal output needed |

Always set maximum output tokens explicitly in production deployments rather than accepting an undefined default.

---

### Safety Settings
**What they are:** Safety settings are configurable filters that control what types of content the model will generate or engage with. They are separate from the model's general knowledge — they are a filtering layer that can intercept or decline to produce certain categories of content before it reaches the user.

**Categories typically covered:**
- **Hate speech:** Content promoting hostility toward groups based on protected characteristics
- **Harassment:** Content targeting or demeaning specific individuals
- **Sexually explicit content:** Content of an explicit sexual nature
- **Dangerous or harmful content:** Content that could facilitate serious harm (weapons, violence, etc.)
- **Deceptive content:** Impersonation or deliberately misleading content

**Why they are configurable:** Different deployments have legitimately different content requirements. A children's educational platform needs the strictest possible filtering. A medical information service needs to discuss symptoms, conditions, and treatments that might trigger overly aggressive consumer-grade safety filters. A cybersecurity research tool needs to discuss vulnerabilities in ways a general consumer product should not. Configuring safety settings to match our deployment context and audience is a legitimate and necessary step.

**Safety setting threshold levels:** Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform exposes safety settings per harm category, typically with threshold levels: Block Few, Block Some, Block Most. Each level adjusts the sensitivity of the filter for that category.

**Business implications for leaders:**

- Review default safety settings for any AI deployment and verify they are appropriate for our specific use case and audience
- For consumer-facing applications, more conservative settings reduce legal and reputational risk
- For internal enterprise tools serving professionals, settings can often be calibrated for professional-context content
- Documenting our safety configuration choices matters for governance, compliance, and audit purposes
- Safety settings should be part of our AI governance policy — decisions made intentionally and documented, not left to individual developers' discretion

---

### Output Verbosity — Prompt-Level Control
Beyond the technical maximum token count, output length and style are also shaped by explicit instructions in our prompt. The technical limit is a safety cap; prompt instructions shape the intended response:

- "Respond in exactly 2–3 sentences"
- "Provide a comprehensive analysis with supporting reasoning — this is for an internal research report"
- "Be as concise as possible — this will display in a mobile notification"
- "Write a full document, minimum 800 words"
- "Use bullet points, not paragraphs"

**Match output style to our application:** A dashboard widget needs concise outputs. An internal research tool may benefit from detailed responses. A customer-facing chatbot typically needs responses that feel conversational — not so short as to be unhelpful, not so long as to be overwhelming in a chat window.

The combination of technical token limits (guardrail) and prompt instructions (intent) gives us precise control over response length and format.
